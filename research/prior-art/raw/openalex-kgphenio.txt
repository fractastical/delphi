    "abstract": "Abstract Model organisms carry decades of systematic gene-phenotype annotation, but connecting those annotations to human disease is rarely straightforward. Phenologs offer a principled way to bridge this gap. A human disease and a model-organism phenotype are phenologs when their associated genes, linked by orthology, overlap more than expected by chance, so the genes of one become candidates for the other. Here we compute phenologs from the Monarch Knowledge Graph, which integrates gene-disease, gene-phenotype and orthology data for human and seven model species. Using PANTHER protein families as orthogroups and a 5% false discovery rate estimated from 1,000 randomized networks, we identify 61,243 phenologs between 3,752 human diseases and 9,700 model-organism phenotypes. We introduce a hypergeometric scheme for ranking the candidate genes a disease’s phenologs nominate and compare it with the naive Bayes scheme of the original phenolog studies. Both methods recover held-out genes within a disease at similar rates, but only the hypergeometric score can be compared across diseases. Using a one-year-older release of the graph, the top 100 candidates per disease recovered 9 of 73 gene-disease associations added since, 23.5 times the random-gene expectation. Mouse accounts for most of these, with zebrafish, worm and fission yeast each contributing associations that mouse does not. A structured analysis of existing resources is necessary to characterize the resources and methodologies needed to facilitate mechanistic inference. Results: Here we demonstrate the potential of knowledge graphs to hypothesize plausible mechanistic accounts of host-microbe interactions in disease and define the need for semantic constraint in doing so. We constructed a knowledge graph of linked microbes, genes and metabolites called MGMLink, and one of microbial traits, environments, and human pheno-types called KG-microbe-phenio.
