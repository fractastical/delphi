**Exploring mathematical biology theories**

I find the idea of "major thinkers" in mathematical biology quite broad. They propose claims that are testable and could potentially evolve into theorems. Theorems exist within formal models, and empirical validation sets them apart. If I consider ranking criteria on “most testable hypotheses that may reach theorem status,” I think of Turing's work on diffusion-driven instability in reaction-diffusion systems and Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection, both offering precise definitions and models that can be tested.

1. Alan Turing — Interacting morphogens can spontaneously break symmetry and generate biological patterns. Reaction–diffusion parameters predict measurable wavelengths, onset conditions, and perturbation responses. Diffusion-driven instability is already proved within broad PDE models; organism-specific applicability remains empirical. Caveat: mechanics, growth, and gene networks can produce similar patterns.

2. Robert May — Large, strongly connected random ecological communities tend to lose local stability. Interaction matrices and perturbation responses can be estimated experimentally. The claim is essentially proved for specified random-matrix community models, with generalizations ongoing. Caveat: real food webs are structured rather than random, which can reverse the prediction.

3. W. D. Hamilton — A social trait is favored when \(rb>c\). Relatedness, fitness costs, benefits, and allele-frequency changes are measurable. Hamilton’s rule is exactly derivable under precise population-genetic or regression formulations; broader causal interpretations remain contested. Caveat: decomposing fitness into \(r\), \(b\), and \(c\) can be model-dependent or statistically tautological.

4. Ronald Fisher — Natural selection increases mean fitness at a rate connected to additive genetic variance. Both variance and short-term fitness change are estimable. Fisher’s Fundamental Theorem is proved under formal assumptions; environmentally and frequency-dependent extensions remain open-ended. Caveat: total mean fitness need not increase when ecology or the environment changes.

5. John Maynard Smith — Evolutionarily stable strategies resist invasion by rare alternatives. Invasion fitness and strategy frequencies are experimentally testable, especially in microbes and behavioral systems. ESS conditions are proved within evolutionary-game models; finite-population and stochastic extensions are only partly general. Caveat: an ESS may be unreachable, nonunique, or irrelevant when mutation and drift dominate.

I deliberately left out Charles Darwin: foundational and highly testable, but his central claims become theorem-like only through later mathematical formalization.
