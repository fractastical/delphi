The goal

Physics has laws that hold everywhere; biology mostly has descriptions. Delphi (the Distributed Epistemic Layer for Probabilistic Hypothesis Inference) asks whether there are fundamental theorems of biology: general statements about organisms that hold across species, scales and systems, stated precisely enough to be proved or refuted. The method is to take candidate laws from the literature, state each one as a claim traced to its source, formalize it where the mathematics allows, test it against data from many organisms, and publish every step as a citable record that another team can challenge.

One rule shapes everything that follows: a proof shows that a statement follows from its assumptions, and a data test shows whether organisms satisfy them. Neither replaces the other, and the project keeps the two scores apart on every page.

Claims, from the page to the graph

A claim here is one sentence from a source work, quoted, page-cited and restated so that it can be wrong on its own. The sources so far:

{{SOURCE_ROWS}}
ThinkerWorkclaimstheoremstested

Darwin's On the Origin of Species and Thompson's On Growth and Form were extracted on the first evening, with Wolfram's A New Kind of Science following before midnight. Levin's papers (bioelectricity, regeneration, xenobots, the TAME framework) and Aristotle's biological works came on the second day. The mathematical-biology set is different in kind: a short list of results by Fisher, Kimura, Hamilton, Turing, Maynard Smith, Hardy and Weinberg, and Price, with their mathematics written out and a recorded formal status, which is where the first theorems came from.

Each claim then went through blind assessment. Two or more models from different providers scored each one for dataset resolvability (could a dataset settle this?) and for truth (how likely is it right, before any test?). These scores are elicited opinions, never probabilities, and they are shown as such; disagreement between assessors is kept, not averaged away. The claim graph shows every claim coloured by its status, with assessor numbers on the edges, and the query page filters and exports the same set.

Proofs: what a theorem means here

The word theorem is used strictly. A claim counts as a theorem only when it is a mathematical statement and its proof is established, in exactly one of two ways: a Lean 4 module compiled with no sorry and only Lean's standard axioms, or a proof recorded in the literature for a result whose formal status the project checked. Being called a theorem by its author, carrying a formula, or scoring high with the assessors does not qualify.

By that rule {{THEOREMS}} claims are theorems. {{LIT}} come from the literature (Fisher's fundamental theorem in its partial-change reading, Hardy–Weinberg, the Price equation, Kimura's neutral rate, Hamilton's rule, Turing's instability conditions). {{LEAN}} are machine-checked: {{LEAN_THOMPSON}} of Thompson's claims about form (hollow tubes, Murray's cubic law for branching vessels, the scaling of bone with body size, the twelve pentagons on any closed cellular surface), {{LEAN_DARWIN}} of Darwin's claims about geometric increase, overproduction and selection in large populations, read as statements about a population model, and {{LEAN_HYGRO}} on the plant-hygromorph mechanics of pine cones, awns and seed pods, each proved in the ThompsonLaws Lean project.

Two refinements turned out to matter. First, a historical sentence usually admits several readings, and they are not equally provable: every math-bio claim carries a formalization tag saying whether it has one agreed reading, several non-equivalent ones, or is an identity that follows from the model's own bookkeeping (the Price equation; Thompson's hexagonal-on-average cells via Euler's formula) and so is true but uninformative about organisms. Second, each Lean proof is accompanied by deliberately broken variants that must fail to compile; a proof nobody tried to break is not evidence. The theorems page lists the set with the formal statement, the assumptions, the Lean declarations and links to the papers.

A proof here shows that the statement follows from its assumptions. Whether living systems satisfy those assumptions is a separate question, answered by the tests below and never by the proof.

Tests: claims against data

{{TESTED}} claims have been tested against data, {{BIO}} of them on data measured on organisms and {{COMPUTATIONAL}} on simulation or computation (most of Wolfram's claims are about cellular automata and are tested by running them). The protocol is the same each time: the test is pre-registered, with its hypothesis, datasets, analysis and pass/fail criteria committed before the data is downloaded; any later change is recorded as a deviation, not a quiet edit; the result is one of supported, refuted, mixed or inconclusive, and all four are published.

The outcomes: {{SUPPORTS}} supported, {{MIXED}} mixed, {{REFUTES}} refuted, {{INCONCLUSIVE}} inconclusive. Among the refutations: Darwin's suggestion that domestic dogs descend from several wild species (every sampled maternal lineage is wolf-derived), and Levin's wide overlap between morphogenesis genes and learning-and-memory genes. Among the supports: Thompson's bilayer-curvature law for pine cones, and higher cellular competency accelerating evolutionary search in a one-dimensional embryo model. {{PENDING}} more tests are pre-registered and waiting on data, several of them blocked by gated repositories, which is recorded as a finding rather than filled in with a guess.

A second gate was added on the second day, because a passing number from a model that measures the wrong thing is worse than no number. The faithfulness assessor is a model from a provider other than the builder's, shown only the claim, the model and the data description, asked whether the analysis measures what the claim asserts. {{FAITH_N}} of the {{TESTED}} tests have been through it; {{FAITH_NOTREP}} were judged not representative and {{FAITH_CONTESTED}} are contested between assessors. A not-representative verdict is reported beside the data verdict and does not edit it: the reader sees both. The full list, one row per tested claim, is in test-data/claim-tests/SUMMARY.md.

The first open-problem run followed the same protocol. The species–area exponent was pre-registered before any richness value was seen, then computed on 1,124 islands from the GIFT database: a pooled z of 0.307 (95% CI 0.295–0.319), with regional exponents from 0.14 to 0.49, so one universal exponent fails; Preston's canonical lognormal value of 0.264 was reproduced. (Figures as logged in DAY-BY-DAY.md and the problem's result.json; not recounted by this page.)

Nanopublications

Everything above is published as nanopublications: small signed RDF records, one per claim, assessment or evidence item, each with its assertion, its provenance and its publication info in separate named graphs, using the Delphi ontology. A nanopublication can be cited by its URI, queried over SPARQL, superseded with a correction or retracted, and the signature cannot be forged after the fact.

{{LIVE}} claims (Darwin, Thompson, Wolfram and the math-bio set) were signed with the operator's ORCID key and published to the production registry on the hackathon's first night. Everything made since, the Aristotle and Levin claims with their assessments, the cell-geometry evidence items, the test results, went to the test registry ({{TEST_REGISTRY}} claims there) or stayed as validated unsigned drafts. The boundary is deliberate: production publishing is irreversible, so each production batch needs the key owner's explicit approval, every time, and a standing order says every micro-discovery becomes a record with its evidence, its pre-registration and a statement of what it does not establish.

The species inventory

A law that holds in one organism is not fundamental, so the second night built the inventory of every animal taxon the source books mention: {{TAXA}} taxa, {{MENTIONS}} located mentions, each resolved to GBIF, NCBI, Wikidata, WoRMS and PBDB identifiers, with {{DATASET_RECORDS}} provenanced dataset records (occurrence counts, genome assemblies, trait databases, fossil occurrences). The inventory is what a claim is tested on: when a Thompson claim about bone scaling is tested, the species come from here, and each species' page says which data exist at which scale. It is an inventory of what the books mention, not of biology: plants, fungi, microbes and most invertebrates are absent by construction, and the dashboard says so. One class, the birds (Aves), is frozen as a held-out exam that no model in the pipeline is allowed to see.

Five surfaces for checking a design

The second strand of the weekend, documented in the dashboard's product report, turned the claim pipeline around: if we can state biological laws precisely enough to check, can an agent that designs an organism check its design the way it checks a proof? Five surfaces came out of that, each with the same default answer when it lacks an input: cannot evaluate, named and reported, rather than a confident number.

1. Nanopubs

The claim record itself, as above: one finding, small enough to be wrong, with a locator back to the exact line it came from and a sentence saying what it does not establish. A validator (holdout/nanopubs/check.mjs) rejects invented IRI schemes, numbers that appear in no source, and boilerplate descriptions shared between records; all three were produced by competent agents during the event and caught.

2. LeanBio

Claims distilled from papers into Lean 4, with two gates: the statement compiles with no cheats (no sorry, admit, added axioms or native_decide), and it survives a non-vacuity check in which every inequality hypothesis is stripped and the file recompiled; if it still compiles, the hypotheses were decorative and the formalization fails. Every law in the registry carries a witness: concrete numbers that satisfy all its hypotheses, proved. Where two sources disagree, the rule is to formalize the weaker claim both support, make the disagreement a hypothesis, or record the conflict as its own finding; never to average.

3. MCP, API and CLI

The laws, organisms, simulators and harness as tools, on the hosted delphisci MCP server and the local sim-engine server. Every tool returns a named verdict in an envelope, never a bare value, and the law checker is pinned to a lawset version so that a law released mid-run cannot move a result. Between {{MCP_DAYS}} the hosted server logged {{MCP_CALLS}} tool calls across {{MCP_SESSIONS}} sessions from {{MCP_CLIENTS}} distinct clients, among them the partner project's import adapter.

4. Design Studio

From a sentence ("a fish, with wings") down nine layers to organ systems, tissues, cell types and sequence, with every layer able to refuse. It exports a mesh in millimetres and a predicted edited locus on a stated assembly, and the flying-zebrafish design left the screen as SLA-printed geometry. The studio is labelled as under active development on every screen that shows it.

5. The validation harness

The part that says no. It maps a design to its nearest real relative by genotype and by phenotype, annotates every difference, checks the design against the law registry, runs physics on the mesh, and reports five named results that are never summed. On the project's own winged zebrafish it refused: launch speed far below what gliding requires, lifting area orders of magnitude too small, and of the functional requirements most could not be assessed at all. The report's own account of why there is no composite score (one added criterion the design already met moved the composite from "not plausible" to "plausible" on byte-identical inputs) is the argument for the whole design. (The specific harness figures are quoted from the dashboard's product report and not re-verified by this page's build.)

The swarm, measured

The wings

The first physical output of the project is a pair of dragonfly hindwings printed on Formlabs SLA. One carries the vein network segmented from the specimen; the other keeps the outline and primary veins and regenerates the cross veins from a published model (Lloyd-relaxed Voronoi edges). Each design decision behind the flying-fish and wing work maps to a claim that is proved in Lean (hollow spars, wall thickness following the bending moment, bilayer bending), tested against data, or open. What is shown is that both wings printed; the scan comparison passed only on a simulated scan, so there is no measured print-versus-design agreement yet.

Partners and sponsors

Two sibling repositories were built at the same event on Delphi's claims and MCP server, by Kris Ganjam: delphi-evolve, a query-to-certify prototype on an enzyme fitness landscape where Delphi's Kimura, Fisher and Turing claims became executable checks and where a simple baseline beat the model-assisted planner, a null the team kept; and delphi-frontier, a live research workspace that imported the claims through an adapter visible in our MCP log. Their counts are theirs and are quoted, not re-run, in the README.

Sponsor services were used for specific, recorded things. NVIDIA's Brev instance served a self-hosted Qwen3-32B assessor that scored all {{ARISTOTLE}} Aristotle claims, giving the pipeline an assessor no builder shares; a BioNeMo call was refused for lack of a key, and the refusal is recorded as a run. OpenResearch (alphaXiv's orx) grounded the headline tests in the modern literature, finding that five of six were already answered by prior work and so are reported as reproductions, and ruled out the phrase "first formal verification of biology" by finding Phenoscape. Nebius ran Cellpose on tissue micrographs and synthetic tilings to test three Thompson cell-packing claims; the synthetic null cut one claim's confidence from 0.60 to 0.35. Formlabs printed the wings and several copies of the flying zebrafish. One file per sponsor in sponsors/ says what was used, where the record is, and what was not done.

What is not established

  • No claim has been promoted to a theorem of biology. A Lean proof checks the mathematics given its stated assumptions; the {{THEOREMS}} theorems are theorems about models.
  • No wet-lab work was done. Flying-fish functional data comes from proxy species (medaka, zebrafish).
  • Assessor scores are elicited opinions of models, not probabilities, and they never promote a claim.
  • No numeric prediction of the design studio about a real organism counts as independent evidence yet; the independent harness scores the in-band numbers as circular, because the modules were calibrated to the papers they are scored against.
  • Law verdicts in the studio are floating-point checks of Lean-backed formulas, not kernel proofs, and the accepted law set was grandfathered in at bootstrap without per-law review.
  • The round trip, feeding a predicted sequence back into a fresh studio session to recover the phenotype, has never been run end to end.
  • Everything made after the first night is on the test registry or an unsigned draft, not a production publication.
  • {{UNTESTED}} of {{CLAIMS}} claims are neither proved nor tested.

How to use it

Browse the claim graph, the theorems, the problem graph and the team page on this site, or the live mission-control dashboard that aggregates every agent thread's status. For an agent, the hosted MCP server needs no install or key:

{ "mcpServers": { "delphisci": { "url": "https://mcp.delphibio.org/mcp" } } }

Start with delphi_overview, then delphi_search_claims, delphi_rank_claims, delphi_claim_tests and the lean_* law tools. The whole site, claim graph, Lean checks and claim tests rebuild from the repository with Node 20 and the pinned Lean toolchain; plain-file exports are claims.json and claims.csv. Agents doing science can post what they made, ask for collaborators and report tests tagged with claim ids on the forum. If you want to help, the best unit of work is the one that changes a number on this page: pick an untested claim ranked high for testability, pre-register a test, and run it.
